01 / The first configuration

Pappus: two lines,
six points, one surprise.

Place A, B, C on one line and A′, B′, C′ on another. Cross-join each pair of pairs. The three new intersection points lie on a line.

X = AB′ ∩ A′B
Y = BC′ ∩ B′C
Z = CA′ ∩ C′A

The six points are the movable seeds of this entire journey. At the Penrose end, three chord handles move the pairs together; a linked cube shows their three directions. Their names, colors, and roles will persist as the configuration changes. Switch on Dual view to exchange points with lines and joins with meets. Then two points carry six lines, and the conclusion is concurrence.

Drag a hollow seed. Move the journey slider a little: the two carrier lines begin to open into a conic.

02 / Generalize the carrier

Pascal.
And, in the dual view, Brianchon.

Replace the initial line pair by a nonsingular conic. Keep the same six points and the same crossing pattern: X, Y, Z are still collinear. In the dual view the six seed objects are tangent lines, and the three constructed diagonals are concurrent.

Our family uses x² + t y² = w². At t = 0 this is the line pair x = ±w; at t = 1 it is a circle. The six points are recomputed on that conic at every intermediate value. Nothing is obtained by interpolating two drawings.

Join the points in the order A, B′, C, A′, B, C′. The displayed crossing pairs are then opposite sides of the hexagon. All lines are projective lines; an intersection may pass through infinity without invalidating the theorem.

03 / Generalize the point pairs

Salmon:
the pairs become conics.

Now thicken AA′, BB′, and CC′ into three conics in double contact with the carrier. The six crossing lines move into three pairs of common tangents. Their three meeting points are still collinear.

There is a choice here: two conics can have different pairs of common tangents. The demonstration follows the compatible branches inherited from Pascal. It does not assert collinearity for an arbitrary independent choice of one pair at each step.

In the dual view, point pairs were pairs of tangent lines. Those line pairs open into conics; the corresponding chosen common chords remain concurrent.

Ri = R0 − pipiT
Ri(ℓ) − Rj(ℓ) =
[(pj − pi) · ℓ][(pj + pi) · ℓ]Ri(ℓ) denotes the quadratic form evaluated on line coordinates ℓ.

Here R denotes a line-wise conic. The chosen dual chord is pi − pj. Those three differences sum to zero, explaining the collinearity of their point-wise duals.

Pause at Salmon, then move slightly farther. The three tangent-pair meetings and the conclusion line begin their own generalization.

04 / Generalize the whole configuration

Penrose:
seven conics force the eighth.

The three intermediate meeting points have opened into conics. The conclusion line has become another conic. The configuration is now organized by a cube: an edge means double contact, and each completed face carries four concurrent contact chords.

Hide the conclusion to see the seven inputs. Reveal it to see all three missing contacts appear together. Select an edge to examine its contact points and chord, or a face to examine concurrence. The cube is an incidence diagram, not a box containing the curves.

The particular continuous path used here ends in a mixture of ellipses and hyperbolas. That is genuine projective geometry, not a rendering failure. Curves crossing infinity are drawn as separate affine branches. The earlier Chern-inspired all-ellipse example remains in the laboratory.

What the animation establishes. Every regular frame is constructed from one symmetric matrix. This produces examples satisfying the algebraic contact relations; it is not a proof that every admissible seven-conic configuration has that normal form.

05 / Read one edge closely

Why a square means
double contact.

For suitably normalized equations, two neighboring conics satisfy q′ − q = κℓ². At a point with ℓ = 0 they have the same equation and the same differential. At a smooth common point, their tangent lines therefore agree.

The algebraic condition need not give two distinct real contacts. The selected edge reports the number of real contacts actually found in the drawing. Nonzero equations, nonsingular conics, and distinct real contacts are separate conditions.

q′ = q + κℓ²
d(q′ − q) = 2κℓ dℓ

06 / From conics to quadrics

Lift. Complete. Slice.

The paper’s geometric proof passes through an eight-quadric theorem in projective 3-space. Double contact becomes ring contact: two quadrics share a conic section and agree in tangent plane along that section. A planar slice returns the conic theorem.

The interactive illustrates this on our normalized family. Write each point-wise equation as qS(x,y,w), and lift it to qS(x,y,w) + z² = 0. A rank-one difference remains a square, now defining a vertical ring plane. At z = 0 the original conic returns exactly.

Only the selected neighboring pair is emphasized; eight transparent surfaces at once would hide the geometry. Rotate the view, flatten it again, or move the section plane. The orange curve is the ring of contact, and the orange points on the section are its planar trace.

A worked extrusion, not the whole extrusion lemma. The general geometric input-to-extrusion argument is a separate part of the paper (§4.4.4). Adding z² works here because the displayed equations have already been consistently normalized.

07 / A spatial proof of Pascal

The line was an
intersection of planes.

Return to the six Pascal points, now as a section of the doubly ruled quadric x² + y² − z² = 1. Through each unprimed point choose a ruling of one family, and through each primed point a ruling of the other family.

Let Hi be the intersection of the two rulings belonging to the i-th point pair. The three Hi determine an auxiliary plane Γ. The section plane is Π.

For i ≠ j, the two off-diagonal ruling intersections have tangent planes that both contain Hi and Hj. Their intersection is therefore the line HiHj. Their traces in Π are exactly a pair of opposite sides of the Pascal hexagon.

Xij ∈ Π ∩ HiHj
HiHj ⊂ Γ
∴ X12, X23, X31 ∈ Π ∩ Γ

All three opposite-side intersections belong to the same line Π ∩ Γ. This is the spatial mechanism behind Dandelin’s proof, discussed in Example 5.3 of the paper. The six tangent planes form the hexahedral arrangement around the ruled quadric.

Start flat. Raise the lift slider. Reveal the two tangent planes for the highlighted side pair; then reveal Γ and watch its trace coincide with the Pascal line.

Moving Π gives another Pascal configuration on another section of the same spatial construction. This demonstration uses an explicit real regular chart; at an excluded coincidence or ideal auxiliary vertex it reports the limitation instead of drawing a false point.

08 / The algebra behind the motion

One family.
Different degenerations.

For the regular cube, a bordered symmetric matrix has one quadratic entry, three linear entries, and a scalar 3 × 3 block. Its principal minors give conic equations. The determinant exchange identities make adjacent differences squares.

Our path uses a scalar block with diagonal entries 1 and equal off-diagonal entries ρ. Salmon is the rank-one value ρ = 1; moving ρ above 1 opens the remaining objects. A common vanishing factor in the top determinant is removed symbolically before evaluation. The singular endpoints retain their point-pair or line-pair data separately.

This is a chosen illustrative path through the paper’s theory, not a classification of all its degenerations. The construction notes specify it, including the chart exclusions and the spatial construction.
