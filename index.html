01 / Pappus

Six points.
Three intersections.
One line.

Choose A, B, C on one line, and A′, B′, C′ on another. The three cross-intersections X, Y, Z are collinear.

X = AB′ ∩ A′B
Y = BC′ ∩ B′C
Z = CA′ ∩ C′A

No distances or angles enter the statement. Move any seed point: the orange line still contains all three intersections. Switch to the dual view to exchange points and lines. The conclusion becomes concurrence of three lines.

Drag a hollow point. Then advance the slider: the carrier pair begins to open into a conic.

02 / Pascal · Brianchon

Replace the two lines
by one conic.

The same crossing construction works for six points on a conic. This is Pascal’s theorem.

Read the six points in the order A, B′, C, A′, B, C′. Then X, Y, Z are intersections of opposite sides of the resulting hexagon. The construction remains projective: a point can pass through infinity without breaking the theorem.

In the dual picture, take six tangent lines to a conic. The three diagonals joining opposite vertices of the circumscribed hexagon meet in one point. This is Brianchon’s theorem.

Why Pappus is a special case

A pair of lines is a reducible conic: its equation is ℓ(x)m(x) = 0. Applying Pascal to that quadratic equation gives Pappus’s crossing configuration. The same labels and construction survive while the carrier changes.

03 / Salmon

Now replace
the point pairs.

Thicken AA′, BB′, CC′ into three conics in double contact with the carrier. The crossing lines become common tangents. Choose the compatible tangent pairs inherited from the six labeled points: their three meeting points remain collinear.

There are different possible pairs of common tangents. The choices must be made together, not independently. Dualizing gives three compatible common chords passing through one point.

The proof fits in three differences

In dual coordinates, normalize a split contact branch as Rᵢ = R₀ − pᵢpᵢᵀ. The difference of two equations factors:

Rᵢ(ℓ) − Rⱼ(ℓ)
= [(pⱼ−pᵢ)·ℓ][(pⱼ+pᵢ)·ℓ].

The selected dual chords are p₁−p₂, p₂−p₃, p₃−p₁. They sum to zero, so are concurrent in the dual plane—or collinear points in the original plane. The square-root choices exist over an algebraically closed field; the real picture follows a compatible split branch.

Pass Salmon slowly. The three meeting points and their common line are the next objects to become conics.

04 / Penrose

Seven conics
determine the eighth.

Generic eight-conic theorem

Put conics at seven vertices of a cube. Neighboring conics have double contact. On each of the three completed faces, the four contact chords are concurrent.

In general position, there is a unique eighth quadratic equation, up to scale, completing the three missing contact pencils and the three remaining face concurrences.

Hide the orange completion to see the seven inputs. Reveal it: all three missing contacts appear together. Click an edge to isolate its conics, or a face to reveal the four chords and their common point.

The cube records which conics are neighbors; the conics do not live on its faces. Its framed-vector mode also controls a local coordinate chart of the configuration. Move a hollow corner to change the geometry, or drag a face to turn the camera.

General-position hypotheses

The seven input conics are nonsingular. The three base contact chords are independent. The initial face-concurrence points lie off the base conic; each opposite conic differs from the base. The three points determined by the known upper chords are noncollinear. These conditions concern only the seven inputs.

The conclusion is a nonzero quadratic equation with proper contact pencils. On regular branches with split chord sections, the new contacts are ordinary two-point tangencies. Conic nonsingularity and two distinct real contact points are separate conditions.

05 / A short proof of the local contact rule

A square records
two tangencies.

If q′ = αq + βℓ², then on the chord ℓ = 0 the two equations and their differentials agree up to scale:

q′ = αq + βℓ²
dq′ = αdq + 2βℓ dℓ.

At either smooth intersection of the conic with the chord, the tangent lines agree. Conversely, two distinct contacts between distinct nonsingular conics give such a rank-one pencil relation.

The chord may have complex rather than real intersection points. The ordinary real tangencies are visible in the selected-edge view; the algebraic contact rule is the same.

06 / One dimension higher

Two points of contact
become a ring.

Penrose’s spatial argument replaces conics by quadrics. Two quadrics in ring contact share a conic curve and have the same tangent plane along it. A plane section recovers the two conics and their two contact points.

Here the already normalized equations lift from q(x,y,w) to q(x,y,w) + z². A difference βℓ² remains a square in space. Its zero plane carries the orange contact ring. Slice at z = 0 to return to the original diagram.

Drag the spatial scene to rotate it. Move the slice. Choose Flat and In space to see the same geometry from the two viewpoints.

What the spatial view shows

This is a worked lift of the normalized family, displaying the rank-one relation and its planar sections. The paper’s general extrusion lemma and eight-quadric existence proof contain additional steps. The eight-conic theorem used here is established by the independent generic algebraic argument.

07 / Pascal in space

The Pascal line is
the meeting of two planes.

The hyperboloid x² + y² − z² = 1 has two families of straight rulings. Through each unprimed seed use one family; through each primed seed use the other. The three paired ruling intersections H₁, H₂, H₃ lie in a plane Γ.

The section plane Π carries the Pascal hexagon. For each pair i,j, the two relevant tangent planes meet in HᵢHⱼ. Their traces in Π are opposite hexagon sides. Their intersection is therefore on both Γ and Π.

X, Y, Z ∈ Γ ∩ Π.

That is Dandelin’s spatial explanation: three planar intersections lie on a line because they are traces of the three sides of one spatial triangle.

Reveal the rulings, then the tangent planes, then Γ. Change the section height without changing the spatial construction.

08 / Explore the family

One picture is
only one configuration.

A framed parallelepiped is a local controller: its three edge vectors carry nine numbers. A generic reconstruction recovers a projective conic configuration from them. Camera motion leaves the conics unchanged; moving the vectors relative to the reference axes changes them.

Wander chooses nearby admissible targets and reconstructs the conics during each transition. Undo restores the last edit. Reframe makes the current configuration the reference cube without changing its curves. This is a bounded local chart, not a uniform sampler of the entire moduli space.

There is another way to organize incidence: replace the cube by a tiled surface. That is the next chapter.
