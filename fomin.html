The main theorem

Fomin–Pylyavskyy’s surface incidence theorem

Let \(K\) be a field, \(V\) a vector space over \(K\), and \(\Sigma\) a finite closed oriented quadrangulated surface with black–white vertex coloring. Label black vertices by points of \(\mathbb P(V)\) and white vertices by hyperplanes. Every edge joins nonincident labels. On each face, the two point labels are distinct.

For a face with cyclic labels \((A,H_\ell,B,H_m)\), call the face coherent when \[ \overline{AB}\cap H_\ell\cap H_m\ne\varnothing. \]

If every face except one is coherent, the remaining face is coherent too. Writing \(\mathcal F\) for the faces and \(\mathcal C(f)\) for coherence, \[ \begin{gathered} \bigl(\forall f\in\mathcal F\setminus\{f_0\},\;\mathcal C(f)\bigr)\\ \Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal C(f_0) \end{gathered}. \]

The notation and the local scalar

Choose nonzero vector representatives \(a,b\in V\) of \(A,B\), and nonzero covectors \(\ell,m\in V^*\), so that \(H_\ell=\mathbb P(\ker\ell)\) and \(H_m=\mathbb P(\ker m)\). The edge hypotheses say \[ \ell(a),\;\ell(b),\;m(a),\;m(b)\ne0. \] Distinct points mean that \(a,b\) are linearly independent. Coherence is equivalent to \[ r_f=\frac{\ell(a)m(b)}{\ell(b)m(a)}=1. \] The ratio is independent of the representatives. The theorem applies in arbitrary projective dimension; the pictured point–line configuration is its planar case.

01 / The dictionary · §2

The edge is not
the incidence.

A tile has alternating labels \((A,\ell,B,m)\). Its four edges record non-incidences. The incidence is the extra assertion that the join of the point labels contains the intersection of the line labels:

\[N=\ell\cap m,\qquad N\in\overline{AB}.\]

The proof surface and the projective configuration are different pictures. Select a tile: its two points, two lines, and asserted incidence are highlighted in the geometry above it. Black vertices denote points; hollow vertices denote lines.

Select tile 1 of the cube. Its labels are \((A_1,c,A_2,b)\): it says that \(b\cap c=O\) lies on \(A_1A_2\).

02 / Desargues · Theorem 3.1

Six faces of a sphere.
Two perspective triangles.

Desargues’ theorem

Let \(A_1A_2\), \(B_1B_2\), and \(C_1C_2\) pass through one point \(O\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}A&=B_1C_1\cap B_2C_2,\\B&=A_1C_1\cap A_2C_2,\\C&=A_1B_1\cap A_2B_2\end{aligned}\]

are collinear.

Use the paper’s cube-shaped tiling of the sphere. Its point labels are \(A_1,A_2,B,C\); its line labels are \(a_1,a_2,b,c\), where \(a_i=B_iC_i\), \(b=B_1B_2\), and \(c=C_1C_2\). Four faces are coherent by construction; a fifth is the assumed concurrence at \(O\).

The sixth face is \((B,a_1,C,a_2)\). Its coherence says exactly that \(a_1\cap a_2=A\) lies on \(BC\). Desargues is the last-face conclusion.

Move \(O\) or a triangle vertex. Select the known faces, then the orange last face.

The outside is a face, too

The nested-square map draws the cube’s surface in the plane. The region outside the large square closes through the point at infinity of the surface drawing. It is not an extra region of the projective configuration. All six tiles are selectable, including this outside face.

03 / Pappus · Theorem 3.2

Nine tiles of a torus.
Three concurrences.

Pappus in the paper’s form

For six generic points \(P_1,\ldots,P_6\), any two of these conditions imply the third:

\[\begin{gathered}P_1P_2,\ P_3P_4,\ P_5P_6\text{ meet at }A,\\P_2P_3,\ P_4P_5,\ P_6P_1\text{ meet at }B,\\P_1P_4,\ P_2P_5,\ P_3P_6\text{ meet at }C.\end{gathered}\]

Construct \(A\) and \(B\) from two of their respective lines, and set \(C=P_1P_4\cap P_2P_5\). Introduce \(a=BC\), \(b=AC\), and \(c=AB\). Figure 9 uses these three auxiliary lines and the six points as its labels.

Six tiles express construction identities. Two encode the assumed concurrences at \(A\) and \(B\). The last, \((P_3,a,P_6,b)\), forces \(P_3P_6\) to contain \(a\cap b=C\).

The dashed hexagon sides are cuts, not tiling edges. Match opposite cuts to form the torus. A tile crossing a cut appears in two pieces; selecting either highlights both. There are nine quadrilateral tiles, and no \(a\! -\! b\) edge.

Drag \(A,B,P_1,P_3,P_5\); the other points preserve the first two concurrences. Tiles 7–9 cross the cuts.

Where are the two lines of familiar Pappus?

Dualize. The first two concurrences become triples of collinear intersection points on two carrier lines; the third becomes the usual collinearity conclusion. Dual geometry exchanges points and lines in this same configuration, not in an independently chosen example.

04 / Complete quadrangle · Theorem 3.3

Five prescribed sections.
The sixth is forced.

Take four points \(A_1,\ldots,A_4\), their joins \(\ell_{ij}=A_iA_j\), and a transversal \(h\). Put \(P_{ij}=\ell_{ij}\cap h\). Choose \(B_1\), then \(B_2\in B_1P_{12}\), and define

\[\begin{aligned}B_3&=B_1P_{13}\cap B_2P_{23},\\B_4&=B_1P_{14}\cap B_2P_{24}.\end{aligned}\]
Complete quadrangle theorem

The last join has the prescribed section as well: \(P_{34}\in B_3B_4\).

Figure 15 is another nine-tile sphere, not the Pappus torus. Five faces are construction identities; three use collinear triples of section points. The last says \(m_{13}\cap m_{23}=B_3\) lies on \(B_4P_{34}\), where \(m_{ij}=B_iB_j\).

Move \(B_1\), or slide \(B_2\). The orange final join still returns to \(P_{34}\).

05 / The same tiles give more · Theorem 3.4

Release a hypothesis.
Keep the proof.

The tiles never used all six section points being on one line. Only three collinearities were needed. Choose \(P_{14}\in\ell_{14}\), \(P_{24}\in\ell_{24}\), and \(P_{34}\in\ell_{34}\) independently, then put

\[\begin{aligned}P_{12}&=\ell_{12}\cap P_{14}P_{24},\\P_{13}&=\ell_{13}\cap P_{14}P_{34},\\P_{23}&=\ell_{23}\cap P_{24}P_{34}.\end{aligned}\]

The same construction still gives \(P_{34}\in B_3B_4\). The same nine tiles prove it, with no extra face and no new cancellation identity.

Move the release slider from \(0\) to \(1\). The section points leave their single line, but the three needed collinearities and the last incidence remain.

Conversely, Theorem 3.6 specializes the complete quadrangle by identifying \(P_{12}=P_{34}\) and \(P_{14}=P_{23}\), yielding the harmonic-points configuration. A single proof diagram organizes both a classical theorem and its stronger relatives.

Theorem 3.4 and Remark 3.7. The release is a continuous geometric construction, not a crossfade.

06 / The proof · Theorem 2.6

Every edge appears
twice, oppositely.

Orient the faces consistently. Every point–hyperplane pairing appears once above and once below the fraction bar in

\[\prod_{f\in\mathcal F}\frac{\ell_f(a_f)m_f(b_f)}{\ell_f(b_f)m_f(a_f)}=1.\]

If all other face ratios are \(1\), the last must be \(1\). Proposition 2.5 translates that back into the required incidence. The work in each application is choosing the tiling and its labels.

Double an edge in the exact-weight view. Two ratios change reciprocally; their product stays exactly \(1\). This isolates cancellation: arbitrary weights are not claimed to be geometric pairings.

Why a coherent face has ratio one

For independent representatives \(a,b\), set \(x=\ell(b)a-\ell(a)b\). Then \(x\ne0\), \(\ell(x)=0\), and

\[m(x)=\ell(b)m(a)-\ell(a)m(b).\]

The join meets both hyperplanes precisely when the products agree. This works in every projective dimension.
