Theorems and sources.

The precise mathematical results behind the three chapters.

1. The three P’s

Pappus and Pascal. The classical library works with arbitrary homogeneous coordinates over a commutative field. Pascal assumes a nonzero homogeneous quadratic equation containing all six points; reducible conics are allowed. Nonzero-intersection certificates make its conclusion a genuine projective incidence. The line-pair specialization is now a direct deduction from Pascal, not a separate polynomial coincidence.

Brianchon. Dual Pascal, together with inverse polarity and a first-order tangent expansion, proves the statement for six polar tangents of a nonsingular symmetric conic in characteristic different from two.

Salmon. The short proof chooses compatible square roots in three contact pencils and writes the dual conics, up to nonzero scale, as Q − pᵢpᵢᵀ. The difference chords p₁−p₂, p₂−p₃, p₃−p₁ are concurrent. On each chord the two conics have the same section; in the primal plane these section points represent common tangents. The arbitrary-contact normalization is proved over an algebraically closed field. A real configuration requires the split choices and distinct real tangent pairs shown in the illustration. Arbitrary independent choices of common tangent pairs are not claimed to satisfy Salmon.

Generic Penrose. Seven nonsingular conics are supplied with nine proper contact edges. The three initial chord covectors are independent; each completed face is concurrent off the initial conic; no opposite-face conic is projectively equal to the initial one; and the three upper concurrence points are noncollinear. The theorem derives the matrix normal form from these inputs and produces a unique projective, nonzero symmetric eighth equation with the missing contact relations and concurrences. A second entry point derives contact relations from two distinct given tangency points. The theorem does not assert that the output is always nonsingular, or that the newly constructed contacts are real. The interactive explores a regular real family.

The spatial illustrations. Pascal’s theorem is formally proved by its conic-determinant identity. New spatial lemmas prove the ruled-quadric equation, its tangent-plane incidence, and the plane-section collinearity argument illustrated by Dandelin’s construction. They do not yet constitute a separate end-to-end proof lifting every arbitrary Pascal configuration. The conic-to-quadric scene has a checked normalized rank-one lift; it illustrates ring contact, not the full eight-quadric extrusion proof.

Reference: Arnold, Chern, Eide, Gunn, Neukirchner and Penrose, Penrose’s eight-conic theorem, especially the introduction, §§4–7 and Example 5.3. Classical vector identities: Maddux, Formulas generalizing Pappus and Desargues.

2. Incidence on surfaces

A finite bicolored quadrilateral gluing records actual black vertices, white vertices, edges and oriented face slots. Label black vertices by nonzero point representatives in a vector space over a commutative field and white vertices by linear forms. All adjacent evaluations are nonzero. The two point representatives on each face are independent. A face is coherent when the line they span has a nonzero vector annihilated by both forms.

The checked geometric surface theorem: coherence on every face except one implies coherence on that last face. This is proved in arbitrary ambient dimension by the local pairing-determinant equivalence and finite edge cancellation. No topological manifold-recognition theorem is necessary; the finite incidence and orientation data carry precisely the gluing used by the proof.

The point–line cube illustrates Desargues and the paper’s seven-to-eight construction. Desargues itself is independently formalized. The surface implication on already supplied labels must not be confused with an existence theorem for filling arbitrary partially labeled surfaces. The displayed arbitrary integer-weight mode illustrates the scalar proof only; not every such assignment is asserted to have a point–line realization.

Reference: Fomin and Pylyavskyy, Incidences and tilings, Proposition 2.5, Theorem 2.6 and Proposition 9.1.

3. Conic contact on surfaces

Every vertex carries a nonsingular conic. Adjacent conics have proper contact relations Qᵥ = λQᵤ + μℓℓᵀ with λ, μ and ℓ nonzero. On each face, the specified first two adjacent chord covectors are independent. Concurrence on every face but one forces concurrence on the last.

The formal input contains the conics, edge contacts and well-definedness hypotheses—not the desired holonomy condition, a global matrix parametrization, or a supplied concurrence for the final face. A tangency-input variant derives the pencil relations from two distinct given contact points, in characteristic different from two.

No bipartition is required for this conic theorem: the new oriented-dart formulation assigns one conic type to every vertex. The earlier bicolored version is retained for comparison with Fomin. The proof establishes uniqueness, reversal and representative-rescaling of λ; then four-chord concurrence if and only if face holonomy is one; finally global cancellation. It is a theorem about contacting conics on arbitrary finite oriented gluing data, not just the illustrated torus.

Local geometric equivalence · Sixteen-conic rational example · Uncolored theorem and boundary formula · Odd-cycle rational example

Boundary version. For any set of faces, its total holonomy equals the product along its induced boundary; all internal edge occurrences cancel. For a coherent patch the combined boundary product is 1. With several boundary components, this does not assert that every component individually has product 1. The theorem is independent of the patch being simply connected.

The examples. The periodic torus family is generated by rank-one increments and matrix inversion. The chosen exact rational instance is independently verified and also instantiated in Lean; its sixteenth concurrence follows from the general theorem. A second exact example has twelve conics on a 3 × 4 torus: not_bicolorable proves its graph has no black–white coloring, and twelfth_face derives the final concurrence from the other eleven. The slider-driven twelve-conic illustration uses the same periodic rank-one method but different numerical parameters; unlike the sixteen-conic example, its face concurrence points need not all be distinct. The H = 2 quadrilateral is an exact Lean-checked example of four proper contacts without concurrence. The parameter sliders and drawing coordinates are numerical implementations, not proof oracles.

What is not claimed. The conic theorem is a nonlinear counterpart of Fomin’s surface theorem. Penrose supplies a compatible local completion operation. We have not proved that arbitrary Fomin labels are degenerations of conic labels, or that a single existence theorem fills every partially labeled surface.

Attribution and the novelty question

The theorem and proof have been developed in this project. Their correctness is supported by Lean verification; this does not settle historical priority. In particular, the rank-one description comes from the classical contact geometry used by Penrose, and the cancellation mechanism comes from Fomin–Pylyavskyy.

Closely related literature includes Bobenko–Fairley, Nets of lines with the combinatorics of the square grid and with touching inscribed conics, and Affolter–Fairley, Discrete Koenigs nets, inscribed quadrics and autoconjugate curves. Those formulations concern inscribed/touching conics and quadrics and their consistency. A full comparison is still required before claiming the present arbitrary-surface, double-contact formulation is new. It is not presented here as an established new result in the literature.
