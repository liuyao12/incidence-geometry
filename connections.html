The main theorem

The conic-contact surface theorem

Let \(K\) be a field with \(\operatorname{char}K\ne2\), and let \(\Sigma\) be a finite closed oriented quadrangulated surface. Assign a nonsingular conic \(C_v\subset\mathbb P^2(K)\) to every vertex \(v\). Adjacent conics are in proper contact along a chord \(\ell_e\). On every face, a specified pair of consecutive contact chords is distinct.

For a face \(f\) with consecutive chords \(\ell_{f,0},\ldots,\ell_{f,3}\), write \(\mathcal C(f)\) for their concurrence: \[ \mathcal C(f)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \bigcap_{i=0}^{3}\ker\ell_{f,i}\ne\{0\}. \]

Concurrence on every face except one forces concurrence on the remaining face: \[ \begin{gathered} \bigl(\forall f\in\mathcal F\setminus\{f_0\},\;\mathcal C(f)\bigr)\\ \Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal C(f_0) \end{gathered}, \] where \(\mathcal F\) is the set of faces.

No black–white coloring is required. All vertex conics and edge contacts are given; this is a compatibility theorem, not a theorem constructing a missing conic.

Proper contact and the meaning of concurrence

Choose nonsingular symmetric matrices \(Q_v\) for the conics. Along an oriented edge \(u\to v\), proper contact means \[ \begin{gathered}Q_v=\lambda_{uv}Q_u+\mu_{uv}\ell_e\ell_e^{\mathsf T},\\\lambda_{uv},\mu_{uv}\in K^\times,\quad \ell_e\ne0.\end{gathered} \] The chord is the projective line \(\mathbb P(\ker\ell_e)\). The intersection in the definition of \(\mathcal C(f)\) is taken in \(K^3\): a nonzero vector in it represents a common projective point. Distinct consecutive chords make that point unique when it exists.

When each chord section splits into two distinct points, proper contact is ordinary double tangency. Real splitting is not needed for the matrix formulation. The algebraic symmetric-matrix theorem is also proved over arbitrary fields; the characteristic restriction here is for the usual conic interpretation.

01 / The surface theorem

Four chords per face.
One face forced.

Conic-contact surface theorem

Assign a nonsingular conic to each vertex of a finite closed oriented quadrangulation. Adjacent conics are in proper double contact. On each face, a specified adjacent pair of contact chords is distinct.

If the four contact chords are concurrent on every face except one, they are concurrent on the remaining face.

No black–white vertex coloring is needed: both ends of an edge carry conics. This is a theorem about conics and their original contact chords—not arbitrary scalar edge values, and not only conics produced by one global matrix formula.

Turn the torus. Select a face: its four labels become four conics in the large drawing. Click one edge value to inspect its two contacts. Reveal the concurrence on the selected face.

02 / Read the contact

Each edge carries
a relative scale.

Choose homogeneous quadratic equations for the conics. Proper contact along ℓ means

\[ \begin{gathered}Q_v=\lambda_{uv}Q_u+\mu_{uv}\ell_e\ell_e^{\mathsf T},\\\lambda_{uv},\mu_{uv}\in K^\times.\end{gathered} \]

The coefficient \(\lambda\) is unique. If two choices differed, a nonsingular rank-three matrix would be a difference of two rank-one matrices. That is impossible.

Reversing the edge replaces \(\lambda_{uv}\) by \(\lambda_{vu}=\lambda_{uv}^{-1}\). Rescaling the equation at a vertex changes neighboring edge values, but does not change the conic. Around a face, the scale changes cancel.

Change Equation scales. The numbers on the four edges move; the conics, chords, concurrence point and face product do not.

03 / The short proof

Travel around
one face.

Multiply the relative scales along the oriented boundary of a face. Call the product \(H_f\). Returning with \(H_f=1\) is exactly the condition that the four contact chords are concurrent.

\[ \begin{gathered}H_f=\prod_{i=0}^{3}\lambda_{v_iv_{i+1}},\\v_4=v_0,\\H_f=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathcal C(f).\end{gathered} \]
Why the equivalence holds

Substituting the four contact relations around a face gives

\[ \begin{gathered}(1-H_f)Q=\sum_{i=0}^{3}\nu_i\ell_i\ell_i^{\mathsf T},\\\nu_i\in K^\times.\end{gathered} \]

If the chords meet at \([x]\), applying this identity to \(x\) gives \((1-H_f)Qx=0\). Nonsingularity implies \(Qx\ne0\), so \(H_f=1\).

Conversely, \(H_f=1\) gives a relation among four rank-one squares. Three independent chord covectors would make the sum of their three nonzero weighted squares invertible. But it equals a single rank-one square. Therefore the chords span at most a two-dimensional covector space and have a common nonzero annihilator.

On a closed oriented surface, each edge is traversed once each way:

\[ \prod_{f\in\mathcal F}H_f=1. \]

If all but one face have \(H_f=1\), so does the last. The local equivalence turns that scalar balance back into geometric concurrence.

04 / More than a cube

A torus of
contacting conics.

The example has sixteen conics and thirty-two contact edges. Every face has four distinct contact chords, and the sixteen faces have distinct concurrence points. All contacts in this chosen real family split into two real points.

The torus is the gluing diagram. It is not a surface on which the conics are embedded. Moving the controls changes actual quadratic equations and recomputes their contacts; changing the torus camera does neither.

Fifteen concurrences can be checked directly. The surface theorem proves the sixteenth. Nothing in the proof depends on the torus having been drawn in three-dimensional Euclidean space.

05 / A boundary formula

Cut out a patch.
Keep its boundary.

The same argument works before the surface closes. Multiply the face transports in any patch: each internal edge cancels with its reverse. Only the boundary remains.

\[ \prod_{f\in\mathcal R}H_f=\prod_{e\in\partial\mathcal R}\lambda_e^{\varepsilon_e}. \]

Here \(\mathcal R\) is the chosen set of faces and \(\varepsilon_e=\pm1\) records the induced boundary orientation. A patch of coherent faces therefore has total boundary transport \(1\). For a patch with several boundary components, the statement concerns their combined oriented product.

Open Explore the boundary of a patch and choose 2 × 2 patch. Four internal edges disappear from the product; eight boundary edges remain. Whole surface leaves no boundary at all.

06 / No coloring required

Three steps
around the torus.

The twelve-conic example has a loop of three edges, so its vertices cannot alternate black and white. Yet its quadrilateral faces obey the same contact and concurrence rule. A separately specified exact rational example is checked in Lean: eleven face conditions are verified, and the twelfth follows from the theorem.

The cut-open view shows the gluing clearly. Opposite sides with matching colors represent the same edges. The opposite boundary occurrences refer to the same labeled edges.

07 / Why the face condition matters

Double contact alone
is not enough.

Four conics may have double contact around a quadrilateral without their chords meeting. In the second example, the accumulated scale is \(H_f=2\). The first two chords intersect, but the remaining chords do not go through that point.

This is not a counterexample to the surface theorem: it is one noncoherent face, not a closed surface whose other faces satisfy the hypotheses.

08 / Fomin and Penrose

A compatibility law.
A completion rule.

Fomin–Pylyavskyy’s theorem uses point–hyperplane pairings. The conic theorem uses the scale supplied by double contact. Both turn geometric face conditions into a product equal to \(1\), then use oriented edge cancellation.

Penrose provides the complementary existence result: on a generic three-face cube patch, construct the missing eighth conic. Where that completion is regular, the opposite three-face patch gives another coherent configuration with the same boundary data.

The surface theorem assumes its vertex labels are already supplied. It does not claim that every partial conic labeling can be completed, or that every Fomin configuration has already been realized as a degeneration of conics.
