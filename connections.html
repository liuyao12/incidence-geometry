The main theorem

Conic-contact surface theorem

Let \(K\) be a field with \(\operatorname{char}K\ne2\), and let \(\Sigma\) be a finite closed oriented quadrangulated surface. Assign a nonsingular conic \(C_v\subset\mathbb P^2(K)\) to every vertex \(v\). Adjacent conics are in proper contact along a chord \(\ell_e\). On every face, a specified pair of consecutive contact chords is distinct.

For a face \(f\) with consecutive chords \(\ell_{f,0},\ldots,\ell_{f,3}\), write \(\mathcal C(f)\) for their concurrence: \[ \mathcal C(f)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \bigcap_{i=0}^{3}\ker\ell_{f,i}\ne\{0\}. \]

Concurrence on every face except one forces concurrence on the remaining face: \[ \begin{gathered} \bigl(\forall f\in\mathcal F\setminus\{f_0\},\;\mathcal C(f)\bigr)\\ \Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal C(f_0) \end{gathered}, \] where \(\mathcal F\) is the set of faces.

No black–white coloring is required. All vertex conics and edge contacts are given; this is a compatibility theorem, not a theorem constructing a missing conic.

Proper contact and the meaning of concurrence

Choose nonsingular symmetric matrices \(Q_v\) for the conics. Along an oriented edge \(u\to v\), proper contact means \[ \begin{gathered}Q_v=\lambda_{uv}Q_u+\mu_{uv}\ell_e\ell_e^{\mathsf T},\\\lambda_{uv},\mu_{uv}\in K^\times,\quad \ell_e\ne0.\end{gathered} \] The chord is the projective line \(\mathbb P(\ker\ell_e)\). The intersection in the definition of \(\mathcal C(f)\) is taken in \(K^3\): a nonzero vector in it represents a common projective point. Distinct consecutive chords make that point unique when it exists.

When each chord section splits into two distinct points, proper contact is ordinary double tangency. Real splitting is not needed for the matrix formulation. The algebraic symmetric-matrix theorem is also proved over arbitrary fields; the characteristic restriction here is for the usual conic interpretation.

01 / From the first chapter

Keep Penrose’s face.
Change the question.

In Penrose’s theorem, the cube is partly labeled: seven conics and the contacts between them are given. The theorem supplies the missing conic \(C_{123}\). The four contact chords around each newly completed face are concurrent.

Now start with all eight nonsingular conics and all twelve contacts already supplied. Forget the concurrence on one face. Can the five others recover it? The surface theorem says yes:

\[ \begin{gathered} H_1H_2H_3H_4H_5H_6=1,\\ H_1=\cdots=H_5=1\\ \Longrightarrow\quad H_6=1. \end{gathered} \]

This is exactly Penrose’s face geometry, but a different implication: seven conics determine an eighth is a completion theorem; five coherent faces force the sixth is a compatibility theorem.

Select any face and reveal its concurrence. The conics use the same opening family and projective viewpoint as chapter 1; the dashed curve is \(C_{123}\). Turn on All conics in context to recognize the whole configuration.

The cube is a quadrangulation of a sphere. Nothing in the new implication requires this particular sphere: the same face geometry can be organized on other closed oriented quadrangulations.

02 / From the second chapter

Keep Fomin’s rule.
Change the labels.

Fomin–Pylyavskyy’s theorem labels alternating vertices by points and hyperplanes. In the plane, a tile is coherent when the join of its two points contains the intersection of its two lines. Our tile has conics at all four vertices, and its four contact chords must concur.

Point–line geometry

Along an edge: non-incidence, \(\ell(a)\ne0\).

A coherent face:

\[ \overline{AB}\ni H_\ell\cap H_m. \]

The local test:

\[ r_f=\frac{\ell(a)m(b)}{\ell(b)m(a)}=1. \]

Conic-contact geometry

Along an edge: proper double contact.

A coherent face:

\[ \bigcap_{i=0}^{3}\ker\ell_{f,i}\ne\{0\}. \]

The local test:

\[ H_f=\prod_{i=0}^{3}\lambda_{v_iv_{i+1}}=1. \]

For Fomin, a nonzero point–hyperplane pairing supplies the edge value. For conics, double contact supplies a relative scale between the two equations. In either case, reversing the oriented traversal uses the reciprocal. The proof closes by canceling each edge against its reverse.

The second chapter’s Desargues example uses the same six-face sphere as the cube above. Its Pappus example instead uses a nine-face torus. Those examples suggest the move beyond the cube; the conic torus below has a different, sixteen-face tiling.

This is a common compatibility mechanism, not a pointwise conversion of an arbitrary point–line configuration into contacting conics. No such conversion is needed for the surface theorem.

03 / The new geometric ingredient

Contact itself
supplies the scale.

The rank-one relation from the first chapter’s contact rule is

\[ \begin{gathered} Q_v=\lambda_{uv}Q_u+\mu_{uv}\ell_e\ell_e^{\mathsf T},\\ \lambda_{uv},\mu_{uv}\in K^\times. \end{gathered} \]

The coefficient \(\lambda_{uv}\) is unique. Two different choices would express a nonsingular rank-three matrix as a difference of two rank-one matrices. This is impossible. The edge value therefore comes from the conics themselves, not from additional arbitrary weights.

Reversal gives \(\lambda_{vu}=\lambda_{uv}^{-1}\). Changing representatives by \(Q_v\mapsto s_vQ_v\) changes the value by

\[ \lambda_{uv}\longmapsto\frac{s_v}{s_u}\lambda_{uv}. \]

All vertex factors cancel around a face. Thus its transport \(H_f\) is independent of the equations chosen to represent the conics—just as Fomin’s ratio \(r_f\) is independent of point and line representatives.

Move Equation scales, then press Follow scale. Edge values change without moving a conic. The accumulated scale still returns to \(1\).

04 / The bridge in the proof

A conic lemma.
A surface cancellation.

The local geometric statement is the essential new step:

\[ \begin{gathered} H_f=1\\ \Longleftrightarrow\\ \text{the four contact chords concur}. \end{gathered} \]
Why the equivalence holds

Substituting the four contact relations around a face gives

\[ \begin{gathered} (1-H_f)Q=\sum_{i=0}^{3}\nu_i\ell_i\ell_i^{\mathsf T},\\ \nu_i\in K^\times. \end{gathered} \]

If the chords meet at \([x]\), applying this identity to \(x\) gives \((1-H_f)Qx=0\). Nonsingularity implies \(Qx\ne0\), so \(H_f=1\). The concurrence point need not lie off the conic.

Conversely, \(H_f=1\) gives a relation among four rank-one squares. Three independent chord covectors would make the sum of their three nonzero weighted squares invertible. But the relation makes that sum a single rank-one square. Hence the four covectors span at most a two-dimensional space and have a common nonzero annihilator.

Now the second chapter’s proof applies unchanged: orient all faces consistently; each edge occurs once in each direction; hence

\[ \prod_{f\in\mathcal F}H_f=1. \]

When every other face has transport \(1\), the remaining transport is \(1\) too. The conic lemma turns that scalar conclusion into concurrence of the original four chords.

05 / Beyond Penrose’s cube

From six faces
to sixteen.

The torus has sixteen vertex conics, thirty-two contact edges, and sixteen faces. All its contacts in this chosen real family have two distinct real contact points. Each face has four distinct chords and its own concurrence point.

The statement now reads: fifteen concurrences imply the sixteenth. Unlike the cube, this surface has a handle. As with Pappus’s torus in chapter 2, the topology organizes the proof, not the location of the geometric objects.

The conics still lie in \(\mathbb P^2(K)\), not on the torus. As on the Fomin page, the gluing diagram opens as a flat periodic rectangle: opposite sides with matching arrows are identified. 06 / A local and global connection

Exchange a cube patch.
Keep its boundary.

Penrose’s cube has two complementary three-face patches. One surrounds \(C_{\varnothing}\), the other \(C_{123}\). Their common boundary has the six conics \(C_1,C_2,C_3,C_{12},C_{13},C_{23}\) and the same six contact edges.

Under the generic hypotheses of chapter 1, Penrose constructs the second patch from the first. When the resulting conic is nonsingular, both patches are admissible for this chapter’s theorem. The boundary formula explains the compatibility they share:

\[ \begin{gathered} \prod_{f\in\mathcal R}H_f\\ =\prod_{e\in\partial\mathcal R}\lambda_e^{\varepsilon_e}. \end{gathered} \]

Interior edges cancel. A coherent patch has total boundary transport \(1\). The two cube patches traverse their common boundary in opposite directions, so their boundary products are reciprocal—and both equal \(1\).

Below the cube, choose Around the base and Around the eighth. Each has three internal edges and the same six boundary edges. This switches the inspected patch; it does not perform a new completion computation.

The same formula works for any selected face patch, including torus patches. For several boundary components it controls their combined oriented product, not each component separately. Choosing Whole surface leaves no boundary and recovers the main theorem.

07 / A difference from point–line labels

No alternating
vertex types.

Fomin’s labeling requires black–white vertices because points and hyperplanes alternate. Here every vertex carries a conic. Reversal is already built into contact transport, so no vertex coloring is required.

The twelve-conic torus has a three-edge cycle and cannot be two-colored. Its faces nevertheless obey the same theorem: eleven concurrences imply the twelfth. The exact rational instance is checked in Lean.

08 / Why coherence is additional geometry

Contact alone
is not enough.

Four conics can be in proper contact around a quadrilateral without their chords being concurrent. In this example the transport is \(H_f=2\), not \(1\). Two chords intersect, but the other two do not pass through that point.

This is a single noncoherent face, not a closed surface with every other face coherent. It shows why the local face condition matters, on top of the edge contacts.

09 / The three results together

Completion and compatibility
are complementary.

Penrose: construct a missing conic in a generic cube. Fomin–Pylyavskyy: propagate point–hyperplane incidence around a closed surface. The conic-contact theorem: propagate Penrose’s face-concurrence condition around such a surface, without needing an alternating vertex coloring.

What is proved here is the third statement and its boundary law. It does not claim that every partial conic labeling can be completed, nor that all of Fomin’s geometric configurations arise as degenerations of conic nets. The new connection is the geometric interpretation of the surface transport.
